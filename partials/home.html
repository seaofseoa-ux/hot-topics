The Value of Money

Money was never supposed to be the point. It was invented to make trade easier — a stand-in for the wheat, the labour, the trust between two people who wanted to exchange something fairly. Somewhere along the way, the stand-in became the goal itself, and that shift is worth questioning.

My own view is that money is best understood as stored time and stored trust, not as wealth in itself. A dollar in a bank account represents hours someone worked and a promise that those hours can be redeemed later for something else of equal worth. When that promise weakens — through inflation, instability, or simple mismanagement — the number in the account stays the same while its meaning quietly erodes. That is why chasing a bigger number without asking what it can still buy, or what it costs a person in time and stress to earn it, is a shallow way to measure a life.

None of this means money doesn't matter. Financial security removes a huge source of daily anxiety and opens doors that goodwill alone cannot. But treating it as the finish line rather than a tool tends to leave people wealthier and no happier. The healthiest relationship with money looks less like accumulation for its own sake and more like using it deliberately: covering real needs, buying back time, and being honest about the difference between being comfortable and being consumed by the pursuit.

GMO Food

Few topics generate as much reflexive fear as "genetically modified" food, and I think most of that fear is aimed at the wrong target. Humans have been altering the genetics of crops for thousands of years through selective breeding; modern genetic engineering is a faster, more precise version of something we have always done. The corn, wheat, and bananas on a grocery shelf today already look almost nothing like their wild ancestors.

Where the conversation gets legitimate is not the science itself but the systems around it: who owns the patents on a seed, whether a handful of companies can concentrate control over what farmers are allowed to plant, and whether long-term ecological effects of specific traits are being studied honestly rather than dismissed. Those are real governance and economic questions, and they deserve scrutiny that has nothing to do with whether the DNA was edited in a lab.

My take is that GMOs, evaluated case by case, have delivered genuine benefits — crops that resist drought, need fewer pesticides, or carry more nutrients in regions where malnutrition is common. Rejecting the entire category on principle throws away tools that could help feed a growing population under a changing climate. The smarter position is to demand transparency and independent testing for each modification, not blanket suspicion of the method itself.

The View of World in 2100

Predicting a century out is mostly guesswork, but the trend lines already visible today give some honest hints. Cities will almost certainly be denser and greener out of necessity rather than idealism — coastal populations pushed inland, infrastructure rebuilt around extreme heat and unpredictable weather, and energy systems that lean heavily on renewables simply because they will be the cheapest option by then, not just the cleanest.

I expect the biggest change won't be visual, though. It will be in how work and identity are organized once automation and AI systems handle a much larger share of routine cognitive and physical labour. That shift could go two very different directions: a world with more free time and broader access to resources, or one with sharper inequality between people who own the systems and people displaced by them. Which path we get depends far more on policy choices made in the next few decades than on the technology itself.

If there's a reason for optimism, it's that humans are unusually good at adapting once a problem becomes undeniable. The world of 2100 will probably look stranger and more fragile in some ways than we imagine, and more resilient in others. Betting entirely on doom or entirely on utopia both seem like ways of avoiding the harder work of shaping the outcome now.
